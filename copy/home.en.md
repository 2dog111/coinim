## Stage two {#stage-2}

Companies and addresses Companies, named people, and addresses that actually receive post. A market is not a mailing list. Stage two turns one into the other, and most of it is done by hand. For each company we find the person who owns the problem rather than the person whose title sounds nearest to it. Then we find where that person's post physically lands. A registered office is not an answer. It is often an accountancy firm two streets away who will bin your envelope without opening it, and they will do it efficiently. Every address is confirmed against at least two independent sources. Where the sources disagree we either resolve it or delete the record. Records do get deleted, and the list you receive is shorter than the list we started with. A letter that arrives at the wrong desk costs more than a short list does, because printing was never the expensive part. ## Stage three {#stage-3}

The letter The letter, and the thing inside it. Something solid in the envelope changes the pile it lands in. It does not go in with the invoices. It gets opened, because nobody can tell what is inside without opening it, and because a shape in an envelope is a small unanswered question. That is the entire trick of the object, and it lasts about ten seconds. After that the letter is on its own. So the letter opens by naming something true and specific about the recipient's operation: a sentence that could not have been posted to anyone else. Not a greeting, not a summary of their industry, not a paragraph about us. If the first sentence would survive a find-and-replace of the company name, it is the wrong first sentence and it gets rewritten. One page. One ask. The ask is small enough to answer on the day it arrives: a reply, a short call, a sample sent back. We do not ask a stranger for an hour of their week. The object is chosen for the argument, not for the budget. The expensive one is often the wrong one. We have talked clients out of the object they liked most, because it made the letter about us, and a letter about us is a letter about nothing. ## The objects {#objects}

Twelve objects, and the argument each one carries Every campaign carries one object. It is the reason the envelope gets opened, and the reason the letter is still on the desk on Thursday. Nobody who receives one mistakes it for a gift. Twelve are shown here, each with the opening of the letter it was built for. They are examples rather than a catalogue: the right object is the one that carries your argument, and if none of these does, we make something else. Every letter below is real work, written to be sent. Key For access, a closed opportunity, real estate, or negotiations. A key opens nothing by itself. What it sets is the meaning: there is a door, there is access, there is something not everyone is allowed into. In a letter, the key says: this is not a mass mailing. It is an invitation through a specific door. Coin For letters about money, profit, savings, and cost of error. Money in the hand works harder than the word “money” on a page. A coin-shaped token sets the subject: this is about real money, not an abstraction. An old-looking token or unusual coin-shaped piece works especially well when the subject is inflation, devaluation, financial memory, rarity, a nonstandard solution, or the cost of delay. Shredded banknotes For a budget spent in the wrong direction: expensive leads, an advertising line nobody can defend, a channel that stopped working two quarters ago. Shredded currency is sold legally by the mint that destroyed it. It is money that is no longer money, which is exactly the argument. A coin says this letter is about value. A packet of shredded notes says something sharper and less comfortable, and it should only be used where the recipient can be expected to agree. It works best when the reader already suspects the waste and has not yet said so to anyone. Blister card For letters that begin with a specific pain: expensive leads, an empty sales pipeline, debt, or overload. The blister-shaped card names the pain before it offers anything. Before the first paragraph, the recipient understands: this is about my headache. A stress card makes the same move differently. It turns pressure into a small physical test. Broken part For letters about repair, replacement, insurance, and quality. A broken thing can make risk more believable than a polished image. The opening can be brutally simple: “I included this broken part for a reason. Big expenses start with small things like this.” First a crack. Then a stoppage. Then urgent repair. Then people explain why no one noticed the problem earlier. In letters about quality, safety, and losses, a broken part does the main thing: it removes abstraction. The error is no longer in the report. The error lies on the table. Photograph For proof: a property, warehouse, production line, or product. A photograph is useful where a claim would be easy to dismiss. It says: this exists. This happened. Not a mockup. Not a promise. Not a polished story from a presentation. For a complex product, a general view is usually weaker than one precise detail: a seam, connection, edge, material, package, finish, defect, or result. Soil or sand For land, beach, resort, and construction. The letter puts a small part of the location into the recipient’s hands. The plot stops being a line in a PDF. The beach stops being a picture. The construction site stops being a promise. Road access, neighbours, footfall, route, future development, and land value stop being abstractions. They become physical context. Material sample For manufacturing, construction, finishing, and packaging. Material is better touched than described. You can write “dense,” “expensive,” “reliable,” “pleasant to the touch” as much as you want. One real sample settles the question faster. Map fragment A map fragment works when the main thing is place, movement, route, or delivery radius. The map forces the recipient to see the situation. Reply card For letters where an answer is needed: qualification, survey, signal of interest, or application. A prepaid reply removes one excuse. The recipient does not have to work out how to answer. A questionnaire or check-style form works even harder: there is an action to complete. It no longer feels like ordinary paper. When a person sends back a form, questionnaire, or short answer, you get a clean signal: they noticed, understood, and acted. And they will almost certainly remember who asked the question. Our card carries three lines and a box beside each. Call me. Not now, try in [month]. Wrong person, try [name]. The third line is the one that pays. A person who does not want the offer usually knows who does, and writing one name is easier than explaining a refusal. A campaign that collects those names ends better routed than it started. Seed packet For a long sales cycle, and for any offer where an honest answer is measured in months rather than weeks. Seeds do nothing for sixty days and then do everything at once. That is the shape of most serious commercial work, and it is the shape most sales letters pretend not to have. Sending seeds says the timeline out loud before the recipient has to ask about it. It disqualifies you with anyone who wants a result this month, which is the point. Those campaigns end badly for both sides. Small hourglass For a deadline that exists whether or not anybody replies: a quarter closing, a budget cycle, a rule taking effect, an offer with a date on it. A timer in an envelope is a strong move and an easy one to ruin. False urgency is obvious and it costs the sender everything. Used against a real date it does something no paragraph achieves: it puts the writer on the clock as well, because the letter now has to earn its reading before the sand runs down. If there is no genuine deadline, this is the wrong object and we will say so. Other objects exist and do not appear here: a feather against a quill, a piece of coal for value that still looks unattractive, a ticket or a blank card where the invitation matters more than the thing. The list grows as campaigns need it to.

## Before a word is read {#before-reading}

Before a word is read An envelope is judged in about two seconds. In the hand, before the eye has done anything useful. By the time the reader has focused on the first line, they have already decided whether this is a document or a piece of post to be dealt with later, which usually means never. Almost everything below is spent on those two seconds. The letter itself only has to survive them. In the hand Weight is the first signal and it arrives before sight. Ordinary office stock is 80gsm and feels like a form. At 120gsm and above the same envelope reads as deliberate. Somebody chose this, and choosing costs money. Nobody consciously notices the difference. Everybody acts on it. It is the cheapest change on this page and one of the two or three that matter most. Then the fingertips. Cotton stock, laid paper with its ribbed grain, sheets with visible linen inclusion: all of these register as personal. A smooth coated surface registers as advertising, because advertising is what it is usually used for. The test is blind: hand somebody two envelopes without letting them look, and they will tell you which one is important. On the outside No window. An envelope with a transparent panel is a bill, and it is sorted into the pile of things to be paid rather than the pile of things to be read. That classification happens before anyone has decided anything, and no amount of good writing inside recovers from it. Not white, and not the standard size. Cream, grey-blue, deep green. A square or an elongated format does not stack neatly, which means it physically protrudes from whatever pile it lands in. It also fails automated sorting, so the piece is handled by people the whole way. Slower, more expensive, and better. A real stamp, stuck on by hand, ideally an unusual one. Metered franking says a system sent this. A stamp says somebody stood at a counter. The difference is visible before the envelope is opened and costs almost nothing to make. On short runs the address is written by hand. This is the strongest signal available on the outside of an envelope and the one that scales worst: four hundred addresses can be written, forty thousand cannot. Where a campaign is small enough, we write them. Where it is not, we say so rather than printing a font that imitates handwriting, which fools nobody and insults the reader who notices. The seal and the impression Where the piece warrants it, the envelope closes with wax. A seal is the only closure that cannot be opened and shut again without saying so, which tells the recipient two things before they read a word: the letter arrived as it left us, and nobody read it first. It also has to be broken with a thumb, which is a physical act performed before any reading has happened. The letter is printed letterpress where the run allows it. Letterpress presses each character into the paper, leaving an impression you can read with a fingertip and see against the light. Digital printing cannot reproduce it. Neither can a competitor working at volume, because the method stops making economic sense somewhere in the low thousands. That is the same threshold where this whole channel stops making sense. Blind debossing goes further: a mark pressed into the sheet with no ink at all. It is read by touch before it is seen. One word, or one device, and nothing more. A page of it becomes decoration, and decoration is what we are trying to avoid. Opening it There is one physical action between the envelope and the letter. A sheet folded in three and tied with thread. A tissue leaf laid over the page. A folder that has to be opened rather than a page that falls out. Each movement of the hand is another second in which the reader is already involved, and involvement before reading is worth more than any opening sentence. Smell works on memory longer than anything else in this list, and it is also the easiest thing here to get badly wrong. Perfume in a business envelope reads as manipulation and is resented. What works is the smell a material already has: cedar shavings, leather, sealing wax, ink still fresh on a letterpress sheet, cotton paper on its own. The scent should be a consequence of what the piece is made from, never an addition to it. Something in the envelope is left unfinished. A line with a figure for the reader to fill in. A tab to be torn. A card to be sent back. An unfinished action stays in the mind longer than a completed one, and a reader who has picked up a pen is no longer reading an advertisement. What is still on the desk on Thursday Paper goes in the bin within the week. A key, a coin, a broken bracket does not. It moves to the edge of the desk, then to a drawer, and every glance at it is another contact we did not pay for. This is the real argument for putting an object in the envelope, and it has nothing to do with the object being clever. What lasts longer than the object is the thing the reader did: broke a seal, untied a thread, wrote a figure, posted a card back. An action performed is remembered far better than a page read. And what lasts longest is the story. A letter that arrives in a way somebody describes to a colleague over lunch has done something no amount of paper stock achieves on its own. That is the only part of this we cannot manufacture, and it is the part we are actually aiming at.

## Why paper, when email is free {#why-paper}

Why paper, when email is free One desk, one working day: 117 emails and 153 Teams messages, and most of the emails are skimmed in under a minute. Microsoft measured that across its own Microsoft 365 telemetry and a survey of 31,000 workers in 31 markets, and published it in June 2025. Post went the other way. American mail volume peaked at 213 billion pieces in 2006. In the year to September 2025 it was 109 billion, a fall of almost half, and it is still falling. Producing a cold email became free, and landing one in an inbox became a specialist skill: warming domains, rotating addresses, writing past the filter. That skill is real, and it is now the price of entry rather than an advantage. A physical letter cannot behave this way, and that is the whole argument. Nobody receives two hundred envelopes in a day, because nobody can afford to send them. Paper costs money. The object inside costs money. Packing, postage and a delivery record cost money. Every one of those costs is a barrier, and the barrier is what keeps the channel worth using. Email lost its barrier and lost its value at the same time. The expense also changes what happens before the send. An email campaign has no reason to verify an address, because a wrong one costs nothing and a bounce is not an event. We check because an unverified record costs us paper, an object, postage and a delivery attempt. We would rather burn a great deal of machine time confirming a record than post one envelope to a company that dissolved last year. That is not diligence for its own sake. It is arithmetic. We collect email addresses in the course of the work. We do not sell them and we do not hand them over, not as a bonus and not on request. What we sell is a street address that a physical letter can reach. If you want an email list there are companies that will sell you one, and the fact that they will is most of the problem.

Deciding who is worth writing to takes as long as that market takes to read. Confirming where those people physically sit takes longer than anyone expects it to. A campaign that fails still narrows the market, which is worth something. Which countries can you post to? Most of them, but not on the same terms. Some routes come back with a signature and a name, some come back with a delivery scan and nothing else, and a few we cannot confirm at all.
