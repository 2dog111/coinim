I built a machine that does the one part of outreach nobody ever does.

This page is the whole story: why the normal way of finding customers is broken, what I built instead, how it works inside, what it cost to build, and what the numbers say. It's long.

Part one

You stopped here:

Part one. Why the normal way doesn't work.

Outreach is done backwards.

Somebody sits down to do cold sales and goes for contacts first. They open Apollo or Clay, type in an industry, set filters on company size, geography, job title, and export ten thousand rows. They think they've defined the market. They've defined their own vocabulary: the database handed back the words they already knew how to type.

With email you can live with that. A send costs nothing, the mistake drowns in the noise, and a month later you don't remember who you wrote to. We work with paper. Paper doesn't forgive.

Paper costs money per piece

Every send is an envelope, paper, printing, an object in the box if there is one, packaging, postage, and hands to assemble it. That's money on the invoice. Then time: paper travels for days.

A confident package sent to the wrong person is a small physical monument to not thinking early enough. It costs money, it travels a week, it lands on the desk of someone who doesn't need it, and you find out about it never.

So you can't write to ten thousand "logistics companies" to see what sticks. You can't even write to a thousand. You have to know who you're writing to before you buy the list, because every line on that list you pay for later in paper.

What convinced me the problem sits elsewhere: I run a channel whose audience is exactly the people my clients want. It generates replies in volume. And even from those, people can't reliably convert to profit. I've seen three hundred leads delivered and zero deals closed. Three hundred and zero. Getting to people was never the bottleneck. Getting to the right people, with the right reason, is.

Paper adds a third word: and there. The right person with the right letter still has to physically arrive in the right building. More on that later.

Which means someone has to do the work nobody does.

The first ten markets are free. The next thirty are the job.

Put ten sharp people in a room, explain the product, ask where it could sell. In an hour you'll have ten directions on the whiteboard. They'll be good ones. Existing customers, competitors' customers, a couple of neighbouring industries, one odd idea somebody remembers from a previous job. Try to make them prove any of it with sources and you'll fail, but the ten are so obvious they're ninety percent right without proof.

The problem is what sits above those ten.

Above them there's another ten, twenty, thirty directions that no living person will ever reach. Every next direction costs exponentially more enthusiasm than the last, and enthusiasm is a finite human resource. I'll be honest: I've never once wanted to sit down and grind through the logic chain for hypothesis number twenty-four. With ten in hand you can spend forever working those ten instead of going wider. Everyone does.

I've tried to buy this work from people. Salaries from three to twelve thousand dollars a month. Weeks and months to get someone to do it even occasionally, even averagely. People will happily do things when you crack the whip. But systematic, grinding, thorough work is against their nature. People think beautifully by feel, in the field. That's exactly why they don't think in systems.

A machine is the opposite. It lives from the moment it wakes to the moment it sleeps, and the systematic nature of a task is the only thing it breathes. It has no mood. Hypothesis twenty-four gets exactly the same attention as hypothesis two, at four in the morning, forever.

Nobody calls themselves what you'd call them

Say you found the market. That still doesn't mean you can find it in a database.

Companies name themselves however they feel like. So do industries. So do people.

Try gambling. The companies call themselves Real Money Gaming. And iGaming. And Casino. And Betting. And eSports. And Monetary Games. Plus whatever category the local regulator invented that you've never said out loud. Type "gambling companies" and you get exactly the narrow slice you asked for.

People are worse. You'll go looking for Founders and classic C-level, and in some parts of the world the person who actually signs partner deals in that industry is called Head of VIP. Head of VIP. Whatever that's supposed to mean. Try typing that into a filter when you've never heard the phrase in your life.

This is where the whole "hire someone with industry experience" thing comes from. Companies pay a premium for salespeople who already learned the vocabulary on someone else's payroll. They're paying for a dictionary somebody else already bought.

Now count what it takes to close that gap honestly. For every segment, every name a company type goes by. For every segment, every title the buyer goes by. Times geography. You get a matrix of tens of thousands of intersections. A person will never bring you that. Not in an hour, not in a day, not in a year. They'll come up with a couple of variants, hammer those, and fold when it doesn't work. That's just how people are built.

And the paper still has to arrive

There's a third hole email people never think about, and for us it's the main one.

Right name, right title, right company. Where do you send it? The database has the headquarters address. Headquarters is a floor of an office building where nobody has ever seen our person. The registered address is worse: it often points to a corporate services firm where somebody's whole job is signing for legal notices and forwarding them on. The person works at a regional site, in another city, sometimes another country. The company moved. The person left. Reception will accept the package and carry it nowhere. A parent company will return mail addressed to an employee of its subsidiary.

Every one of those misses costs exactly what a hit costs. And you won't see it.

So on top of the right market, the right person and the right letter, there's one more step: a proven physical route. A linked record: this company, this person, this role, this site, this mailing address, checked so that every part agrees with every other part. If it doesn't agree, it doesn't go. And it doesn't get tested on your postage.

What follows from that

Add it up.

Every send costs real money and travels a week, so you aim before you buy the list.

Half the market sits above the ten a brainstorm reaches, and no amount of money gets a human up there.

The market you did find, you can't reach if you don't know what it calls itself.

And even the right person won't get the paper if you haven't checked where they actually sit.

Four jobs. Each one needs inhuman patience and zero creativity, just consistency. Exactly what people don't have and a machine has in abundance.

That's where Market Scan came from. It does the part of the work a human won't do because of how humans are built.

Part two. What happens inside.

This isn't one prompt or one model. It's four sessions in a row, each one doing its own job, each one standing on the one before it. Earlier versions were different and worse. That's part three. This is how it works now, and then, past the scan itself, how a map of a market becomes a letter in someone's hands.

The input

Before any of the sessions run, a bot talks to the client. A conversation, not a form. Out of it comes what the business sells, who already buys, where it can operate, what makes the offer worth money, what can't be said, and what decision we ultimately want a stranger to make. Plus the client's domain. That's where the machine starts digging. Plus recordings of calls with customers, if there are any: the most useful thing on the way in and the least often sent. Names can be stripped out. What matters is how the buyer describes the problem in their own words.

Session one. Research.

A swarm of models reads everything that exists on the market around this business. Competitors, customers of competitors, case studies, reviews, partner pages, directories, associations, job postings, regulations, adjacent technologies, and, this matters, whatever people use instead of buying anything at all.

Out of that comes the market chronicle. One document that explains how the market actually works: who has the problem, what they call it, who's already selling into it, where the money leaks, which roles usually own the problem. And, this is what makes it usable, the chronicle marks its own weak spots. What's backed by sources. What's still a guess.

From the chronicle come the hypotheses. The priority set at the top, and every experimental direction that's still defensible underneath.

Where do the hypotheses come from? Three places at once.

The model's own memory. It's read the entire internet, it's seen more than any living person, which is why it answers broad questions instantly, with no reasoning. Ask "who buys this kind of thing" and it sketches the field on the spot.

Deep research. On a given topic it reads hundreds of sources in minutes and finds connections a person wouldn't, because a person can't read that many sources and think hard about them even over several days.

Brand clouds. Take the businesses of this type and the direct competitors of the one we're working for, and look at who's in their customer base. In a competitor's brand cloud there can easily be an entity nobody would ever think of. You just have to find it. The machine finds every customer type of every competitor type and, if the task is set right, decides: is this an anomaly, is it bullshit, or is it real. And puts a percentage on it.

That percentage is the mechanism. Every hypothesis gets a weight, and the weight is a number rather than a yes or a no. Only the ones with a non-zero weight go forward. This is what stops the machine from inventing markets. It can imagine anything it likes, but if the imagination has no evidence behind it, it gets a zero and it stops there.

The hypotheses come in tiers. Tier one, the close obvious ones, the ten from the whiteboard. Tier two, one step sideways: the same problem in a different context, or a partner who buys for a completely different reason than the end user. Tier three, experimental, the ones a brainstorm never reaches: an unusual buyer, an odd application, an organization that can push the offer into many companies at once. Experimental doesn't mean made up. It means the evidence is thinner and the path is longer, so the test is smaller and the bar is higher.

Last thing Research does: it groups. If the product sells to, roughly, any kind of bank, you don't want ten categories of slightly different banks and payment companies. You want one clear vertical: cash custody fintech, say. A vertical is a group of hypotheses that can live on one letter. If the buyer or the argument changes, that's a different vertical.

Up to fifty minutes for all of that.

Session two. Writer.

For every vertical with a non-zero weight, it writes a chain of letters.

Worth stopping here, because this alone is not a human task. Getting people to write a letter for a specific type of client at all is hard enough, however much you pay them. But that's not the job. The job is the grinding, systematic work of thinking through the ideal template: a letter built so that nobody can tell it's a template, and that fits every target title in the chosen cohort so exactly that there's no logical hole to poke. That is the work I couldn't buy from people.

The machine writes chains of unique letters for every segment better than I ever wrote them myself, even if I'd written one a day. A whole pack, in two hours.

The letters are specific because each one is written for a specific vertical with a specific buyer and a specific reason to buy. A warehouse operator, a conveyor integrator and a private equity operations team get different letters, even though all three might care about conveyor failures. They buy for different reasons, they object to different things, different people sign, different evidence works.

Up to fifty minutes.

Session three. Market Arms.

This is what the whole thing was built for. We write to people, and people are named however.

With everything Research and Writer produced under its arm, the next echelon of models goes out and does another global research pass, this time about vocabulary rather than the market. For every segment: what the target employees are called, and what the companies that belong to this segment call and classify themselves.

I told you about gambling in part one: six names for one industry, and Head of VIP as the title of the person who signs partner deals. The machine finds every variant of company-type name inside a target industry, and every variant of job title for the goal and offer type, in an hour or two. And it does that as many times as we have segments and hypotheses in the target.

What comes out are arms. Each arm is a company type, times how that type names itself, times the function that owns the problem, times how that function is titled, plus geography, plus scale. On paper that cross-product runs to tens of thousands of intersections per segment. Most of them collapse into each other or die on evidence, and what survives is dozens of arms on a narrow segment and several hundred on a wide one.

Here is one, written out.

Real Money Gaming operator, calling itself iGaming

Head of VIP, the person who signs partner deals

Malta, one hundred to five hundred staff

Every arm is people the sourcer will go and find. Some arms are thousands of people. Some are dozens or hundreds. And the small one can easily be the better market. In total it's millions of people.

A person will never bring you this table. Part one covered why.

The trick with the machine is that it knows every peculiarity of everything on earth. You just have to know how to ask it, at the right moment, in the right way. And then send it properly to research the current situation in the geography that matters.

Up to fifty minutes.

Session four. Judge.

Checks everything the previous three did against a giant checklist. And can send individual pieces back for rework.

Individual pieces, and that is the point. A specific segment, a specific letter, a specific group of arms. The judge asks: is there evidence, do the hypotheses follow from the research, are similar markets grouped right, does the letter match the buyer, are the arms useful or just numerous.

And there's a principle underneath all of this. It runs with no deterministic constructors, no gates, no brainless script validators. A script can check JSON, arithmetic and file structure. It can't check whether a hypothesis makes sense. So the judge is a model too. Meaning isn't a file format.

Then the paper starts

The scan is done. We have the map of the market, letters for every vertical, and the matrix of arms. Now that has to become an envelope on a specific desk. Here email people would already be sending. We still have three steps ahead, and each one rejects things.

Companies. Every accepted arm becomes an instruction for the sourcer. It brings back candidates. Candidates. Each one gets checked: does the company really do the thing, in that geography, at that scale, does it have the operational signal the hypothesis requires, is it alive, is it a duplicate, is it part of a bigger group, does it trade under another name. Is it an operator, a vendor, a consultant, or a line in a directory. One arm easily returns both a company with eleven sites that calls itself "supply chain solutions" and a broker that calls itself a 3PL, ranks beautifully, and owns no facilities. We take the first and throw out the second. Rejection is worth more than acceptance here: it's the only proof the filter is doing anything.

People. Inside an accepted company we look for the person closest to the decision. Who owns the problem, who feels its cost, who holds the budget, who can sign the next step or carry it to whoever will. A title is evidence. A group VP has authority and zero interest in one line at one site. A site director feels every minute of downtime and can't approve anything across the group. The right person is whoever owns the consequence at the scale you're selling at.

Route. And here we do the thing normal lead-gen systems simply skip. Where the paper physically reaches this person. Every failure from part one applies: the registered address, headquarters in another country, a regional site, a person who left, reception that carries it nowhere. So the route is checked as a linked record: current company, current person, current role, the right site, the physical mailing address, supporting sources, conflicts. Six outcomes: verified, needs review, role uncertain, office uncertain, moved, rejected. Anything short of verified doesn't go into production.

The letter goes last

Because the letter needs everything before it. We already know why this market, why this company, why this person, which problem to raise, what evidence exists, and which building the paper will physically reach.

Only now can a letter be genuinely specific. Each vertical gets its own commercial argument. Each recipient gets one clear reason for the approach. Each letter asks for exactly one next step.

And if there's an object in the box, it carries part of the argument. A worn bearing pulled from a line during an unplanned stop, for the site director who has held exactly that part in his hand on a bad morning. A material sample, when quality or waste shows faster than a paragraph reads. A coin or a banknote, when money should be the subject before the envelope is opened. A map, when there's a route, a network, a territory, a gap. A reply card, when the next action needs to be obvious. The object is a sentence you can pick up.

And none of it needs a button

The scan runs entirely on its own. I don't sit next to it, I don't press anything, I don't know it's running. The client talks to the bot, and a few hours later they have the market chronicle, a set of weighted hypotheses, the verticals, letter chains and a matrix of arms covering millions of people. From there, sourcing, route verification and production run off that map for months.

Five minutes talking to a bot on the way in. On the way out, what used to take between two hundred ninety and five hundred sequential sessions and up to two and a half days for one product. How that got squeezed into four is the next part.

Part three. How I built it.

It started with a dumping folder.

The first version worked like this. I drop the client's domain into a folder, along with a recording of the call in whatever format, as long as whisper can make out our mumbling, and any documents that exist. That's it. Then a dedicated coding session goes off for two days and digs through the market. Every hour it deepens the weighting of hypotheses, finds evidence, builds a map of job titles, ties company size to title to market, takes apart every competitor and every competitor of every competitor, direct and indirect. What comes out is a queue of campaigns that then fight each other on equal send volumes: a hypothesis either earns more contacts and moves up a tier, or loses a few rounds in a row and drops out.

Two days for one business. Slow, but even then I understood what kind of animal this was. McKinsey charges millions for this and takes months. I've held a report in my hands that someone paid a million for. Two million, actually. And it was worse. So much worse that I opened a bottle of whisky that night. Well, I'd have opened it anyway. What's it doing sitting on the shelf.

Then I went in a fourth time

By the time release looked days away, everything I'd been grinding toward for months was done. Full self-serve, an automated seller, plus contacts collected in a way nobody collects them, by the million, better than Clay and Apollo and cheaper. The first client wrote in a week later, and it felt fucking great, even though he was writing about the old revision, and in those few days the letters and the knowledge underneath them had gotten a hundred times stronger.

And that's when I went back in for the fourth time.

The task sounded simple. Make research plus copywriting plus business reasoning run automatically for any task. One model gave me something sane at some point and I thought that was it. Then a new one came out, and the fear that it could be even better wouldn't let go. Several more days, every weekend, sixteen hours a day. And in the middle of it, the new model lost its mind, and I mean that literally: both models I was working with went stupid in front of my eyes, down to complete imbecility. They stopped seeing the details of the task. I'd say anything at all and they'd latch onto it and dance in circles around that one thought. The repo, where working code already sat, they no longer looked at. They'd wait for me to say something and discuss it in a vacuum.

A hammer that changes its molecular composition in your hand. You've got a million nails to drive, and it goes hard rarely, and you have to guess the moment. And only people with a giant repo feel this. It'll build a landing page fine. It'll build a CRM where you drag cards around with a mouse fine. People brag about that on Facebook, though one prompt could do it a year ago. But with a genuinely complex codebase you're in despair, and you're alone. Well. Not alone.

Five in the morning, I'm sitting there, told to fuck off by both major providers, writing a ten-thousand-line prompt by hand. Market scan plus copy plus knowledge. Through hardship to the stars.

That was version four. Version two had already stunned the people I showed results to, about their own businesses. Version three was several times better than two. And for four I'd designed something I thought was final, and I was running hundreds of experiments under it to find the one configuration I could carve in stone.

A month to get stable

A month went into getting the models, working as a team, with no deterministic constructors, no gates and no dumb script validators, to produce a scan that satisfied me. How simple it sounded in my head, and how impossible it turned out to be. The number of parameters that have to get along for it to be both good and stable is beyond belief. I did this thing every hour I was breathing. Waiting for night to push into production a build that had finally shown stability on the test bench, finish ten more little things, and rest. Three days, please.

And then the breakthrough. Quality higher than anything I'd ever done by hand. Even for a hundred thousand dollars I couldn't have bought a year of my own time and attention to produce that width at that quality. And it does it without me entirely. I don't press buttons. I don't even know it's running.

Here's the scale of what had to be squeezed. At the peak, one scan for one product used between two hundred ninety and five hundred sequential sessions. The record run took two and a half days. I burned tens of billions of tokens on the experiments. Then I refined the paperwork, the doctrine and the methodology texts the scan lives on, until four sessions were enough. Research, Writer, Market Arms, Judge. Fifty minutes each.

From five hundred to four, and the quality went up. Because all those months went into one thing: writing down what "good" means precisely enough that one machine can check another and send it back. Everything hard turned out to be in the text, not the code.

The system drank all my blood, and a few other fluids besides. I do this around the clock and I'm sick of it. But I'm not stopping.

What I took from it

Three things.

This is version four, and every version before it already worked and already impressed people. I rebuilt not because it broke, but because I'd figured out how to do it better.

The models shifted under my hands the entire way. The same model that's a genius at research goes insane on code, and which one was best changed every two weeks. That's why, in the benchmark, stability is going to be its own property, worth more than creativity.

And everything that let me squeeze five hundred sessions into four was paperwork. Doctrine. Text.

Part four. The benchmark.

Once I had a build that held stable, one question was left, the one I'd been putting off for the hundredth time: which models to run it on.

Up to then I'd always worked on a specific set of models I believed in. Believing isn't enough when I'm going to sell this for money and answer for the result. I had to run the whole scan, all four sessions, on every model, several times over, and get averages I could call representative. I've done this dozens of times before, so the numbers are real, not "somebody counted something and called it a benchmark."

It took a full day.

What I ran

Five models. Three real clients, data exported read-only from production. Fifteen cells. Offline sidecar, maximum parallelism, production untouched.

Two of the biggest names weren't in this run. I had already dropped them from my roster. But from dozens of earlier experiments I know how they'd have done. One of them was solidly mid on this kind of work: shallow on creative depth in text and meaning. In research tasks it scored five times lower and worse than the best model here. The other was much stronger and runs neck and neck with the fastest model in this test, and those two are the ones actually competing in this niche.

The numbers

Look at the total line per model, all three clients together.

The conservative one: forty-one and a half minutes on average, fifty segments, five hundred seventy-five arms. Doctrine-shaped, safe. That's my current production baseline.

The fast one: sixteen minutes, forty-eight segments, six hundred sixty-one arms. Fastest of all, even maps, controlled copy. Closed every cell in fifteen to seventeen minutes.

The wide one: forty-two minutes, seventy-three segments, four thousand four hundred seven arms. On all three clients it found the widest universe of segments and arms. Slower, and it overproduces arms, and they need measuring and pruning afterward.

The voice one: twenty-nine minutes, thirty-nine segments, twelve hundred seventy-five arms. Strong voice in the letters, wide maps, but capacity-sensitive: on one client the first attempt died on a quota limit and only went through on retry.

The compact one: thirty-one minutes, thirty-nine segments, two hundred thirty-six arms. Finished, but narrow, with a messier route, and one first attempt hit an API inspection wall. Good as an independent sanity check. Not a first fallback.

The number that explains everything

Take one of the three clients. The fast model found twenty-seven segments there. The wide model found twenty-nine. A tie. Anyone comparing models would say: same thing, move on.

Now the arms. The fast model made four hundred sixty-three. The wide model made two thousand three hundred and one.

Same hypotheses. Same market. Five times as many people you can actually reach. And the entire difference is twenty or thirty extra minutes of the model grinding through its reasoning.

That's why I say the gap isn't in understanding the market. Both understood it. The gap is patience with vocabulary. How many names one thing has, in how many places, before the model decides it's found enough names. One is satisfied at four hundred sixty. The other keeps digging to twenty-three hundred.

And a market scan is done once in a lifetime, to plan a year of work. So those extra twenty minutes are a total addressable market four to five times larger for the whole year. By my estimate, an eightfold acceleration of a sales team over a ten-year stretch, for twenty extra minutes of pocket-change tokens. And that's the comparison for beginners: dig into the details and there's more, plus text constructions earlier models couldn't do at all.

How I route them now

Research and adjacent discovery: the wide model, with the fast one as fallback. Wide gives the broadest maps; fast is the quick, controlled backup.

Market Arms: the wide model again, because coverage is maximal. The voice model second, if capacity is healthy. The conservative model for conservative maps, when you want production-shaped rather than maximal.

Letters are the interesting one. I use the conservative model with the fast one as fallback, but the fallback is sneaky: both always write, and the judge picks between them. Thirty percent of the time it picks the fast one, which is a remarkable result for a model that's many months old by now. The voice model is the second voice, the style challenger, when capacity allows.

Emergency fallback for the whole run is the fast model. Cleanest operational profile end to end.

Judge is a separate contract, and I didn't evaluate it in this run. Only writer cells here.

On stability

Separately about the fast model.

It's nowhere near as creative in text as the conservative model. That one can produce things the fast model never will. But it can also fuck up, including simply not answering the API call. The fast model is the most stable model in existence. It delivers its seven out of ten in text so evenly that you could run it a thousand times on a thousand texts and get exactly the quality you expect every time.

For anyone who isn't prepared to write enormous treatises of quality methodology for the runner to lean on, that's not great: seven out of ten is seven out of ten. For someone like me, with a doctrine thousands of lines long, it's fantastic, because predictability is worth more than peaks.

Why the ranking flipped

From outside this looks like a contradiction.

A few weeks before this run, I'd been saying the voice model beat the wide model, and the wide model destroyed one of the big names. In the benchmark, the wide model is the king of breadth and the voice model is second-tier.

The difference is the task. The earlier ranking was about who codes better and holds a complex codebase together. There, the wide model went insane, and I was force-killing it at hour fifteen, hour fifty. On coding tasks it basically never wraps up. I never once waited it out. The benchmark ranking is about research and writing. And on those tasks, strangely, the wide model doesn't go insane: it finishes all the work itself and closes out in forty-two minutes, versus sixteen for the fast one.

One model, two tasks, two different verdicts. If only this thing could code without losing its mind, it'd be priceless.

What the benchmark doesn't prove

I wrote this into the scorecard itself. It answers writer interchangeability. It doesn't prove production routing, doesn't measure paid sourcing, purchased lead quality, deliverability, or judge acceptance. To make this production, it needs a small governed implementation: keep the conservative model as baseline, add explicit routing by stage, keep deterministic validation to JSON, transport and math only, then compare against the judge and run a bounded live smoke.

Part five. The money, and what this actually is.

What it costs next to a human

Start with the simplest arithmetic, the one that's been sitting in my head all summer.

Take one person capable of doing this whole scope perfectly. Find thirty hypotheses instead of ten, prove each one with sources, write a letter chain for each, dig out every company name and every job title for each, put it all into a matrix of tens of thousands of intersections. That person is almost impossible to find, and if you find them, they cost a fortune and take months.

Now take the cost of tokens for the same scope. With the task set right, the machine comes out thousands or tens of thousands of times cheaper. And the better its brain is engineered for the task, the cheaper it gets: fewer wasted runs, fewer redos, fewer sessions. Five hundred sessions versus four is a money number too.

I stress the word brain. The machine has been smarter than all living people combined practically since it appeared. Its only problem is that it doesn't share that mind on its own. There is no wanting in it. I have the wanting. And I have writing, which is how the wanting gets put into it. That's how you get a complete genius: its knowledge, its capacity, and properly sharpened desire, which is my contribution.

Why paper makes all of this mandatory

Lead-gen software got very good at accelerating the part that comes after the hard decision. You can export ten thousand contacts before breakfast. Enrich them before lunch. Generate ten thousand personalized opening lines before dinner. Connect six tools and watch the records move between them at the speed of a factory producing something nobody has checked.

One question remains: why should these people hear from you at all.

Most tools ask for an industry. Then a company size. Then a geography. Then a title. Then, how many contacts do you want. They start after the hardest work has already been silently skipped. They don't ask whether the industry label hides five different buyers. They don't ask who owns the problem in a small company versus a large group. They don't ask whether the company calls itself something you didn't think to type. They don't ask whether the person still works there. They don't ask whether paper can reach them.

The machine accelerates. The thinking stays parked.

With email you can go years without noticing. With paper you can't. Paper costs money. The object costs money. Hands cost money. Postage costs money. A confident package sent to the wrong person is a small physical monument to the decision not to think early enough.

So I build in the opposite order. First understand the market. Then its language. Then find the companies. Then the people. Then prove the physical route. Then write the letter. Then produce anything. Then remember what happened.

Wide in discovery, suspicious before spending

A scan isn't good because it produced a big number. Discovery should be wide. Spending should be suspicious.

Before contacts are bought and letters are produced, the system removes: hypotheses without support, duplicate arms, company names that mostly return noise, titles with the wrong authority, geographies where the offer can't operate, company types that look similar but don't fit, empty legal entities, dead companies, consultants pretending to be operators, marketplace profiles pretending to be companies, and searches too small or too noisy to justify their cost.

The scan widens the world first. Then it cuts away what can't defend itself. A system that only generates is a content machine. A system that can reject its own attractive ideas is starting to become useful.

One continuous record

Usually research lives in a document. Company sourcing lives in a spreadsheet. People live in another spreadsheet. Address verification happens in messages. Copy lives in a separate file. Production happens in a supplier portal. Tracking happens in a carrier account. Replies show up wherever they feel like. Then somebody tries to reconstruct what happened from six tools and the memory of a person currently on vacation.

I'm building around one linked record. Hypothesis, vertical, arm, company, person, physical route, letter, production, dispatch, delivery, response.

Open a company and see why it entered the campaign. Open a person and see why they were chosen. Open an address and see why it was approved. Open a letter and see which market logic produced it.
