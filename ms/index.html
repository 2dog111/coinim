I kept buying answers to the questions I already knew

I opened Apollo or Clay, selected an industry, company size, country and job title, and got a file with 10,000 rows a few minutes later. I looked at the rows and thought I was looking at the market.

I was looking at my own filters. A company using another name for itself stayed invisible. A buyer carrying a title I had never heard stayed invisible too, even while both records sat inside the database waiting for the right words.

Over 17 years I gave LinkedIn $20,196, mostly through Sales Navigator at $99 a month. I cancelled it in January. During the previous 4 years, the channel I had paid more than $20,000 had produced nothing I could seriously call distribution.

I could hide a bad list inside email volume. Paper put it on an invoice.

Paper, printing, the object inside the package, assembly, postage and a week of travel cost me the same whether the letter reached the right person or died at reception.

A confident package sent to the wrong person became a small physical monument to something I had failed to think through. It travelled, landed on a desk that never needed it, and told me about the mistake never.

I have watched one channel deliver 300 leads and produce 0 deals. Three hundred and zero. Reaching a person was only the first condition. The person still needed a reason to care, and the envelope needed to reach the building where they actually worked.

I could only filter with the words the market had already taught me

Gambling made the problem obvious. I found companies calling themselves Real Money Gaming, iGaming, Casino, Betting, eSports and Monetary Games. A local regulator could add another category I had never said out loud. When I typed “gambling companies”, the database returned the narrow slice my vocabulary allowed.

Job titles were worse. I searched for founders and conventional executives. Then I found a market where the person signing partner deals was called Head of VIP. I could not put that title into a filter until the industry had taught it to me.

I ended up seeing industry experience as a dictionary somebody else had already paid to learn. That was the expensive part.

Then the address broke the record anyway

I could find the company and still get headquarters while my person worked at a regional site in another city. The registered address could belong to a corporate services firm. Reception could accept the package and carry it nowhere. Sometimes the company had moved, the person had left, or a parent company returned mail addressed to an employee of its subsidiary.

Every miss cost exactly what a hit cost. I ended up with one rule: current company, current person, current role, correct site, physical mailing address and sources that made those pieces agree. A record that disagreed with itself never entered production.

I had to map the market before I bought another contact

I could put 10 sharp people in a room, explain the product and fill a whiteboard with 10 sensible markets in an hour. Existing customers, competitors’ customers, neighbouring industries and one idea somebody remembered from an old job always arrived first.

The expensive work began above that first 10. I watched hypothesis 24 receive less patience than hypothesis 2 because a person had a mood, a deadline and a finite appetite for grinding through evidence. After working with 680 businesses, I kept meeting the same whiteboard. The first directions were there. The sources under them were not.

I tried to buy this labour from people earning $3,000 to $12,000 a month. Weeks and months passed before the work appeared, occasionally and approximately. People think beautifully by feel and in the field. I needed hypothesis 24 to receive the same attention at 4 in the morning as hypothesis 2.

I start Market Scan with a business file. The intake is manual now: name, work email, company domain, call recordings if they exist, and anything that explains the product and its buyers. Names can be removed from recordings. I need the buyer’s own language, the operating limits and the decision the recipient must eventually make.

I made research show me where its evidence stopped

I sent the research session through competitors, their customers, case studies, reviews, partner pages, directories, associations, job posts, regulations, adjacent technologies and the substitutes people used when they bought nothing.

It returned one market chronicle: who had the problem, what they called it, who already sold into it, where money leaked and which roles owned the consequence. I made it mark every weak spot too. A sourced claim and a direction that still needed proof could no longer sit in the same tone of voice.

I gave every hypothesis a weight. Ideas with no evidence received 0 and stopped. I grouped the survivors into verticals that could live on one commercial argument. When the buyer or the reason to buy changed, I split the vertical.

One separate deep-research job took me through hundreds of London microcement vendors in minutes. It returned names, price ranges, the gap between the bottom and ultra-luxury ends, and what customers said on Reddit. Two hours earlier I could not pronounce the word. Then a contractor asked where I knew all of it from. I needed that width.

I wrote a separate reason to answer for every vertical

I turned every accepted vertical into a chain of letters. A warehouse operator, a conveyor integrator and a private equity operations team could all care about conveyor failures. They bought for different reasons, objected to different things and gave authority to different people. One template could not honestly fit all 3.

My real runs produced lines such as “The license is approved. The bank still says no.” “Where the subscription money lands.” “The fix is live, but not on their phones.” “The one-line compliance fix that weighs 25 MB.” “The 0.4% behind your 1.5%.” “The day the market moves is the day your app gets judged.”

I built combinations that no single filter could express

The next session researched vocabulary. For every segment, I made it look for every name used by that company type and every title used by the function that owned the problem. Then it combined company type, self-description, function, title, geography and scale.

One arm I kept could read like this: Real Money Gaming operator, calling itself iGaming. Head of VIP, the person who signs partner deals. Malta. 100 to 500 staff.

I began the cross-product with tens of thousands of intersections. Most collapsed into one another or died on evidence. I could finish with 50 arms in a narrow segment, several hundred in a wide one, and sometimes 3,000, 5,000, 10,000 or 20,000 across a large scan. The small arm could still be the better market.

I made a judge reject answers that looked good and proved nothing

The fourth session checked the previous 3 against a long contract. It could return one segment, one letter or one group of arms for rework. I made it ask whether the evidence existed, whether the hypotheses followed from the research, whether similar markets were grouped correctly, whether the letter fitted the buyer and whether the arms were useful rather than merely numerous.

A script checked JSON, arithmetic and file structure. It could not decide whether a market hypothesis made sense. I used a model as the judge because meaning is not a file format.

The scan gave me a map. I still had to make the mail arrive.

After the scan, I had the market chronicle, weighted hypotheses, verticals, letter chains and the matrix of search combinations. I kept company sourcing, route verification and production outside that map because each stage could still reject it.

I accepted companies for evidence, not for matching my keyword

I turned every arm into an instruction for the sourcer. Each candidate had to survive the work it actually did, its geography, scale, operational signal, trading names, group structure and current status.

One search returned a company with 11 sites calling itself “supply chain solutions” and a broker calling itself a 3PL, ranking beautifully and owning no facilities. I kept the first and removed the second. The rejection proved the filter was doing something.

I matched the person to the scale of the consequence

A group VP could have authority and no interest in one line at one site. A site director could feel every minute of downtime and still be unable to approve a group-wide contract. I needed the person who owned the consequence at the same scale as the offer and could move the decision or carry it to whoever would.

I checked the physical route as one connected record

I connected the current company, current person, current role, correct site, mailing address, supporting sources and every conflict I could find. A route ended as verified, needs review, role uncertain, office uncertain, moved or rejected. Only verified routes entered production.

I wrote the letter after the market, company, person and route agreed. Each vertical received its own commercial argument. Each recipient received one clear reason for the approach. Each letter asked for one next step.

When an object belonged in the package, I made it carry part of the argument. A worn bearing for a site director who had held the same failed part. A material sample when quality showed faster than a paragraph read. A map when the subject was a route or a territory. A reply card when the next action needed to be obvious. The object became a sentence you could pick up.

I built three usable versions. The fourth forced me to split the machine apart.

I began the first version with a dumping folder. I dropped in the client’s domain, a call recording in whatever format Whisper could understand, and every document we had. One dedicated coding session spent the next 2 days digging through the market.

Hour by hour it deepened the hypotheses, found evidence, built title maps, connected company size to role and market, and took apart competitors and the competitors around them. I already had something useful. I also had something too slow, too expensive and too dependent on one session holding the whole world in its head.

Version 2 stunned the people who saw their own businesses inside it. I made version 3 several times better. Then GPT 5.6 Sol Max appeared, and I could not leave the possibility of another jump alone.

I lost several days, every weekend and 16 hours a day to it. In the middle of the work, the models stopped seeing the actual repository and started circling whatever I had said last. At 18:50 on 13 July, GPT 5.5 and Opus were doing both the design and the implementation noticeably better than Sol Max and Fable 5. I had watched models degrade before. A swing that large inside a week was new.

The hammer was changing its molecular structure in my hand. I had 1,000,000 nails to drive, it became solid occasionally, and I had to guess the moment.

At 5 in the morning I was sitting there, rejected by both major providers, writing a 10,000-line prompt by hand. I put market research, copy and business knowledge into one document. That became version 4.

I spent 8 straight days running nothing else. Then I spent a month making the models produce work that held together without deterministic constructors, brainless gates or a person assembling the answer between steps.

The breakthrough came from the paperwork. I kept writing down what “good” meant until one machine could inspect another machine’s work and return the weak part. Research. Writer. Market Arms. Judge. Each session could take up to 50 minutes.

I went from 500 sessions to 4 and got better output. Everything hard was in the text.

The system drank all my blood and a few other fluids. I am sick of it. I am still building.

I stopped trusting the demo and ran the whole machine fifteen times

Once the build held stable, I stopped accepting belief as evidence. I was going to sell this work and answer for the result. I ran the whole scan on every candidate model several times, with the same inputs and no hints.

I used 5 models and 3 real clients. I exported the data read-only from production into an offline sidecar and left production untouched. All 15 cells completed with valid JSON. The full run took a day.

I left Fable 5 and Opus 5 outside this table. They were not part of this run.

I treated one beautiful run as an anecdote

Qwen could write things GPT 5.5 never would. It could also fail to answer an API call. GPT 5.5 delivered its 7 out of 10 so consistently that 1,000 runs produced roughly the quality I expected each time. With a doctrine thousands of lines long, I valued predictability more than an occasional peak.

The benchmark measured autonomous completion and the breadth of the map. I did not use it to claim production routing, paid sourcing, purchased lead quality, deliverability or judge acceptance. Those still needed governed routing, deterministic checks for transport and arithmetic, comparison against the judge, and a bounded live smoke.

I use Market Scan when one wrong contact costs real money

I built this for people who had already bought a list, written to it and received silence. They already knew the contact was the most expensive part of outreach, especially when every miss also became printing, assembly, postage and a week of waiting.

When the job is to add rows to a spreadsheet, I buy a list. Market Scan earns its place when I need the market, argument, role and route to survive scrutiny while changing them is still cheap.

I keep discovery wide and spending suspicious. I remove unsupported hypotheses, duplicate arms, noisy company names, roles with the wrong authority, impossible geographies, dead entities, consultants pretending to be operators and searches too small to justify their cost before production.

I do not throw the map away after the first 1,000 rows. Weak markets shrink. Strong ones become more precise. New title patterns appear and old addresses die. I change the map when the business, product or evidence changes.

I am building the work around one linked record: hypothesis, vertical, arm, company, person, physical route, letter, dispatch, delivery and response. I can open any part and see why it exists. The CRM remembers how I built the campaign.
